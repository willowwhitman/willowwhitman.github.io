The term

Coercive control: what it is and the five signs

Coercive control is not a character flaw, a run of bad luck, or proof of some defect in the people it happens to. It is a method, with parts, and the parts assemble the same way whether the hand running them belongs to one person in a house or to a state with an intelligence service behind it.

That definition, and everything below it, is drawn from The Machinery of Compliance — a free, source-checked book on coercive control by Willow Whitman, published in full on this site with all 642 of its notes.

The method

The five signs of coercive control

Stripped to its frame, the method is this: cut you off from anyone who could independently confirm what is real; take control of what you are allowed to believe; wear you down until resistance feels pointless; punish you when you step out of line; and build the whole arrangement so that if anyone ever does look, what they see reads as an accident, an illness, your own instability, or a story too far-fetched to take seriously.

  1. Cut you off from anyone who could independently confirm what is real.
  2. Take control of what you are allowed to believe.
  3. Wear you down until resistance feels pointless.
  4. Punish you when you step out of line.
  5. Build the whole arrangement so that if anyone ever does look, what they see reads as an accident, an illness, your own instability, or a story too far-fetched to take seriously.

Four scales

The same method, whoever is running it

The book's central claim is that coercive control keeps its shape from the household to the state. What changes climbing between scales is never the method — only the money, manpower and legal cover available to make it deniable.

Asked and answered

Common questions about coercive control

What is coercive control?

Coercive control is not a character flaw, a run of bad luck, or proof of some defect in the people it happens to. It is a method, with parts, and the parts assemble the same way whether the hand running them belongs to one person in a house or to a state with an intelligence service behind it. Stripped to its frame, the method is this: cut you off from anyone who could independently confirm what is real; take control of what you are allowed to believe; wear you down until resistance feels pointless; punish you when you step out of line; and build the whole arrangement so that if anyone ever does look, what they see reads as an accident, an illness, your own instability, or a story too far-fetched to take seriously.

Is coercive control a crime?

The recognition, in law, of the pattern itself as a crime rather than its separate acts. The UK's Serious Crime Act 2015, s.76, was the first major common-law statute to criminalise controlling or coercive behaviour as a course of conduct — the point where the law began to catch up to the mechanism.

Does coercive control work the same way at every scale?

The book argues that it does, about shape and never about prevalence. These four are parallel scales, not rungs of a ladder. Nothing climbs from one to the next — a household does not progress into a state. Almost every real situation sits at a single scale: one layer, not the whole stack.

How do I recognise coercive control from the inside?

The book's position is that “do I feel abused?” is the wrong question, because the mechanism is built to be invisible from inside it. Recognition rests on structure instead: whether your information is narrowing, whether your memories are being contested, whether closeness arrived faster than intimacy accrues, and whether your account keeps getting pre-empted.

What this book does not claim — about prevalence, about analogy versus documented link, and about guilt beyond the cited public record — is set out at /legal.
