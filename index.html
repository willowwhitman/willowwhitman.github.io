What it is

Coercive control is not a character flaw, a run of bad luck, or proof of some defect in the people it happens to. It is a method, with parts, and the parts assemble the same way whether the hand running them belongs to one person in a house or to a state with an intelligence service behind it.

Stripped to its frame, the method is this: cut you off from anyone who could independently confirm what is real; take control of what you are allowed to believe; wear you down until resistance feels pointless; punish you when you step out of line; and build the whole arrangement so that if anyone ever does look, what they see reads as an accident, an illness, your own instability, or a story too far-fetched to take seriously.

What to do — two things, in this order

First, safety. If there is any risk of physical harm, understand that the most dangerous moment is often not while you stay but when you begin to leave. A safety plan comes before a paper trail. Call your country's helpline from a device the other person cannot reach, before you do anything that could be discovered. Nothing below matters more than this.

Then build a record, because the pattern is the evidence. No single incident will look like much on its own; that is by design. The pattern is what a stranger can finally see, and a pattern only exists if it is written down.

  • Write things as they happen — dated, factual, plain, the way a sceptical stranger would need to read them: what happened, when, and who else saw it. Not how it felt; what occurred.
  • Keep originals — messages, records, documents — and get copies somewhere no one else can reach: not a shared device, not an account someone else has the password to.
  • Already months or years in with nothing written down? You can still rebuild the timeline from traces you never made on purpose — bank and phone records, dates on messages, medical and work files, other people's memories. A gap in your notes is not the end of a record. It is the start of a reconstruction.

The core of it is a single move: a record makes the pattern visible to someone who was not there — on your terms, and in advance, before the story gets decided for you. That is the one thing the machine is built to prevent, which is exactly why it is the thing to do.

“Am I just paranoid?”

This is usually the question people are most afraid to ask. The answer is not yes, and it is not no. The answer is: you check — and the checking is your protection, not a betrayal of your own judgment.

For each thing that feels wrong, ask whether it is truly the pattern, or whether it is ordinary incompetence, coincidence, or someone simply behaving badly — and require something you could show a stranger before you count it. What is real survives that question easily. What isn't, falls away. Either way you end up holding something you can act on, instead of a fear no one else can see.

Do not inflate one bad event into a whole conspiracy; and do not dismiss a real pattern because each piece, taken alone, looks small. Both mistakes end in the same place — with no one able to help you.

It also helps to know that most situations are one layer, not the whole stack. The full chain is rare and expensive, because every layer costs someone money, coordination and time. Count what is actually in front of you rather than assuming the whole machine. One tapped phone is one tapped phone until there is evidence of more.

Everything on this page is stated plainly and asks to be taken on its usefulness. The rest of the book does the opposite — it proves each piece, across four scales, from a single kitchen table to the CIA, out of court records, declassified files and institutions' own reports on themselves. It is built so you can go as deep as you want and stop wherever you need to.

If this page is all you have room for today, you have enough to begin. Take the safety step. Start the record. Come back for the rest when the ground will hold it.

The book

A method, with parts — not a character flaw

Coercive control is a sustained pattern of behaviour — isolation, surveillance, rules, humiliation and threat — by which one party removes another's freedom of action and their ability to check what is real. It is cumulative rather than incidental, and it is built so that no single instance looks like much to an outsider. The same structure appears whether the operator is a partner, a group, an institution or a state; only the resources change.

Coercive control is not a list of cruel acts; it is a working system — an engine, a set of moves, and a signature reversal that recasts the person harmed as the one at fault. One incident at a time it looks like nothing: a comment, a moved bill, a checked phone, a story quietly adjusted. As a pattern it is unmistakable — and that pattern runs, structurally unchanged, from the intimate relationship to the institution to the state.

The Machinery of Compliance takes the mechanism apart layer by layer: how the engine runs, why denial-and-reversal works so well, how surveillance and money become instruments of control, and how the same machinery now operates at population scale through the platforms that decide what we see. It draws only on what can be documented, and it is scrupulous about the line between the established and the merely plausible — naming real harm without ever slipping into the paranoid style.

It does not stop at diagnosis. Because the machine runs on deniability, it can be beaten by a record — contemporaneous, corroborated, and written for a reader who starts out unconvinced. The closing chapters and two practical appendices show exactly how to build one, and where to find help.

For anyone who has been told that what they can plainly see is not real: this book explains the machine — and hands back the pen.

The mechanism, stripped to its frame

Cut you off from anyone who could independently confirm what is real; take control of what you are allowed to believe; wear you down until resistance feels pointless; punish you when you step out of line; and build the whole arrangement so that if anyone ever does look, what they see reads as an accident, an illness, your own instability, or a story too far-fetched to take seriously.

The only thing that changes as the operator gets bigger — from a partner, to a group, to an institution, to a government — is how much money, manpower and legal cover they have to make it deniable. The method itself does not change.

A note on content

This book examines coercive control in close detail, at every scale from a single household to the apparatus of a state. In doing so it discusses domestic abuse, psychological control, torture, homicide, child abuse, suicide and state violence. The treatment is measured rather than graphic, but it is sustained, and some of it is hard to read.

The book does not ask to be believed; it asks to be checked, which is why every claim is sourced and tiered. But checking comes second. If something in your own life is beginning to feel wrong, your safety comes before any paper trail.

Method

Check, don't believe

The authority in these pages is not a résumé. It is a method. Every claim in the book is tiered to the evidence it actually rests on, and the tier is stated where the claim is made — so you always know whether you are reading something established, something assembled, or something contested.

Tier 1

Documented

Established from outside the target's own account — a court file, a declassified document, an official inquiry, a peer-reviewed study, or the operator's own record.

Tier 2

Synthesis

A pattern assembled from separately documented parts. The parts are on the record; the joining of them is the book's argument, and is labelled as such.

Tier 3

Contested

Disputed, unresolved, or plausible but unestablished. Named as contested rather than quietly used as if settled.

What would prove it wrong

The book states its own breaking conditions rather than leaving them to a reader to find: if the pattern turns out to be a technique that spread rather than a shape that recurs; if the categories were drawn from the cases rather than found in them; or if a scale exists without the mechanism. It is also explicit that a silence is not a confirmation.

The categories are the operators' own words — love bombing from the Unification Church, Zersetzung from the Stasi, "disrupt, discredit, neutralize" from COINTELPRO. The claim throughout is about shape, never prevalence.

From the book

Recognising coercive control from the inside

The mechanism's whole design is to be invisible from within it. It does not feel like abuse; it feels like reality, like your own choice, sometimes like love. So recognition cannot rest on how things feel. Feeling fine is not evidence of safety, and feeling crazy is not evidence of being wrong — in the documented cases, both feelings were manufactured on purpose.

Which is why the useful question is not how do I feel about this? but what does the structure show? The book's appendix turns its own diagnostic method inward as a set of structural tests. Answer each from evidence, not recollection.

  1. Is your information narrowing?

    Are you seeing fewer independent sources, hearing one person's account of things before any other, being steered away from people who would give you a second reading? Don't ask whether you feel isolated — write down who you have stopped talking to, and who encouraged each of those distances.

  2. Are your memories being contested?

    Do you keep hearing that didn't happen, you're remembering it wrong? Repetition alone makes such a claim feel truer over time, independent of whether it is true. Test one instance against a dated record.

  3. Is there tempo and asymmetry?

    Did closeness or trust arrive faster and more totally than intimacy actually accrues — and, on reconstruction, was one party building the bond while the other was operating it? The warmth can have been real; the tempo is the tell.

  4. Does your account keep getting pre-empted?

    Do other people already have "the story" about you before you have spoken — a manager, a relative, a mutual friend who heard a version first? Ask who reached them, and when.

A caution the book puts before these tests. A list of the people you have stopped seeing, annotated with who encouraged each distance, is a far more revealing document than the book is — it is evidence that you have started assessing someone, and it names the few contacts you have left. If there is any chance your home, phone or accounts are not private, do this somewhere else, on something that is not yours to be found with, or keep it in your head until there is a safer place to put it.

Building a record, because the pattern is the evidence

No single incident looks like much on its own; that is by design. The pattern is what a stranger can finally see, and a pattern only exists if it is written down. Write things as they happen — dated, factual, plain, the way a sceptical stranger would need to read them: what happened, when, and who else saw it. Not how it felt; what occurred.

The book's closing chapters and two of its appendices set this out in full: how to keep a record that survives contact with someone who starts out unconvinced, and how to do it without the record itself becoming the danger.

Glossary

The vocabulary of coercive control

Twenty terms, defined as the book uses them — which is not always how a dictionary or another field would. Each has a full entry in the book, with an index pointer to every chapter that uses it.

Coercive control
Not a personality flaw or a private tragedy, but a mechanism: isolate a target from anyone who could independently confirm reality, monopolise what the target is allowed to believe, induce helplessness, punish deviation, escalate when threatened, and deny it all in a form built to be unprovable or reclassifiable.
Coercive control as a legal offence
The recognition, in law, of the pattern itself as a crime rather than its separate acts. The UK's Serious Crime Act 2015, s.76, was the first major common-law statute to criminalise controlling or coercive behaviour as a course of conduct — the point where the law began to catch up to the mechanism.
DARVO
Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender: Jennifer Freyd's name for the move in which the person who caused the harm takes the victim's chair, denies the act, attacks the accuser, and recasts the accusation itself as the injury done to him. It works best where there is no fixed record to anchor against.
Gaslighting
The disputing of a person's memory of events, incident by small incident, until they stop trusting their own account. Effective not merely because someone is willing to lie, but because it exploits a real property of memory — that memory is reconstructive and suggestible.
Zersetzung
The Stasi's "decomposition," codified in MfS Directive 1/76: covert operations aimed at secretly destroying a target's self-confidence, reputation, employment and relationships until the will to resist collapsed — all without a visible state hand. An institutional, years-long version of the inescapable-stress condition that produces learned helplessness.
Scale-invariance
The property that the mechanism runs the same way at every scale — one relationship, a closed organisation, an institution that looks away, a state with intelligence tradecraft behind it. What changes climbing between scales is never the method, only how much money, manpower and legal cover the operator has to make it deniable.
Deniability engine
Deniability built directly into the design of an operation, so that whatever is done can be dismissed as an accident, an illness, a delusion, or a conspiracy theory too far-fetched to entertain. Not damage control after the fact, but a structural feature present from the start.
Traumatic bonding
Dutton and Painter's account of what happens when a severe power imbalance meets intermittent reinforcement — cruelty interrupted, at unpredictable intervals, by real kindness. The not-knowing-which-is-coming wires attachment to the source of harm, so the same person registers as both danger and comfort. The bond can outlast the relationship.
Learned helplessness
Seligman and Maier's finding, as reframed by later neuroscience: prolonged, genuinely uncontrollable stress wears down the brain circuit that detects whether a situation is escapable, so that a real exit can go unregistered. It turns why didn't she just leave from a character judgment into a neurological one.
Agentic state
Milgram's term for the felt shift in which a person stops experiencing themselves as the author of an act and begins experiencing themselves as the instrument of someone else's decision. Guilt has nowhere to attach because the self that could refuse has, for the duration, been handed over.
Grammar and vocabulary
What changes between cases and what does not. Vocabulary is the particular instrument an operator happens to use — poison, surveillance, a psychiatric label, a disinformation campaign. Grammar is the constant structure underneath. Different actors speak different vocabularies while running the same grammar.
Claim tiers
The three levels at which the book holds every major claim, never blurred. Documented: settled by a record. Synthesis: a pattern honestly assembled from individually documented components, argued as a case rather than asserted as proven. Contested: genuinely unresolved, often because the mechanism was built to leave no trace.
Analogy
Two instances of the same mechanism that resemble each other because unrelated actors converged on the same small set of methods that actually work — no contact, no coordination. This is the book's default assumption for every resemblance it documents: same method, independent actors, no link assumed.
Homology
A documented, provable connection between two instances of the same mechanism — an actual link established by evidence, not merely the same method appearing independently. Held to a far higher bar than analogy, and asserted only where the evidence forces it.
Illusory-truth effect
The finding that a statement encountered more than once is judged more likely to be true on the strength of repetition alone — and that this operates even against knowledge the person could use to flag the claim as false. The engine under a repeated version of events, a chanted slogan, and saturation propaganda alike.
Misinformation effect
Loftus's documentation that a misleading detail introduced after an event can be folded into memory as though witnessed, and that whole false episodes can be implanted through confident, repeated suggestion from a trusted source. Not the claim that memory is worthless — only that it is reconstructive and suggestible.
Mere-exposure effect
Zajonc's finding that familiarity alone produces a drift toward trusting whatever has become familiar, with no new reason supplied. Repeated, unpressured contact is not a neutral prelude to influence — it is already influence, which is why grooming so often begins with nothing more than presence.
Anchoring and framing
Tversky and Kahneman's finding that the first figure or frame a person is handed silently sets the reference point everything after is judged from, and that identical facts cast as a gain or a loss produce opposite choices. The cognitive basis for deciding the story in advance, then fitting the facts to it.
Herman's three stages
Judith Herman's recovery framework, which cannot be skipped or reordered: first safety, because a nervous system still detecting threat cannot do any other work; then remembrance and mourning, building an honest narrative of what happened; then reconnection, rebuilding relationships, meaning, and the restored capacity to make choices that change outcomes.
Reconstruction
The answer to both believing invisible harm because it would explain everything, and disbelieving it because no one can see it: build the invisible back into visibility through whatever trace it could not help but leave. A well-run operation can hide its cause but not fully hide its effects. Show the mechanism; show what it left behind.

What is coercive control?

Not a list of cruel acts but a working system: isolate the person from anyone who could independently confirm what is real, take control of what they are allowed to believe, wear down resistance, punish deviation, and arrange the whole thing so that if anyone looks, what they see reads as an accident, an illness, or the person's own instability.

Legal

What the book does not claim

A book about deniable harm has to be scrupulous about what it alleges. The book holds itself to the standard below; this site holds itself to the same one.

The book is built primarily from publicly available records — court documents, declassified files, official inquiries, peer-reviewed research and published journalism. Where an individual was charged but not convicted, or was acquitted, that fact is stated. Nothing in the book, and nothing on this site, should be read as an assertion of guilt beyond what the cited public record establishes.

Where two cases resemble each other, the book's default assumption is analogy: unrelated actors converging independently on the same small set of methods that work — no contact, no coordination, no link assumed. A documented connection (homology) is asserted only where the evidence forces it, and is labelled as such where it appears. The book's central claim of scale-invariance is a claim about shape, never prevalence: it does not say that this mechanism is everywhere, that most relationships or institutions run it, or that any particular situation involves the whole stack rather than one layer.

Claims are tiered — documented, synthesis, contested — and the tier is stated where the claim is made. Quoting a synthesis or contested claim as if it were established misrepresents the book. Steps were taken to protect victim anonymity; any resemblance to persons or events beyond the public record is unintentional.
