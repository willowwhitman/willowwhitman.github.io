The Short Version

This is the whole book in one sitting. If you are reading it because something is being done to you that you cannot get anyone else to see, read this page — and if it is all you have room for today, stop here. You will have enough to start. Everything after it is the same thing said at length, with the proof, and you do not need the proof to use the tool.

What it is

Coercive control is not a character flaw, a run of bad luck, or proof of some defect in the people it happens to. It is a method, with parts, and the parts assemble the same way whether the hand running them belongs to one person in a house or to a state with an intelligence service behind it. Stripped to its frame, the method is this: cut you off from anyone who could independently confirm what is real; take control of what you are allowed to believe; wear you down until resistance feels pointless; punish you when you step out of line; and build the whole arrangement so that if anyone ever does look, what they see reads as an accident, an illness, your own instability, or a story too far-fetched to take seriously.

The only thing that changes as the operator gets bigger — from a partner, to a group, to an institution, to a government — is how much money, manpower, and legal cover they have to make it deniable. The method itself does not change. The tables in Map of the Layers, a few pages on, lay this out at a glance, one scale at a time.

What to do

Two things, in this order.

First, safety — before anything else. If there is any risk of physical harm, understand that the most dangerous moment is often not while you stay but when you begin to leave. A safety plan comes before a paper trail. Find your country’s domestic-violence helpline — findahelpline.com and nomoredirectory.org list them by country, and the “Resources and Help” appendix at the back has more — and call it from a device the other person cannot reach, before you do anything that could be discovered. Nothing below matters more than this.

Then build a record, because the pattern is the evidence. No single incident will look like much on its own; that is by design. The pattern is what a stranger can finally see, and a pattern only exists if it is written down. So:

  • Write things as they happen — dated, factual, plain, the way a skeptical stranger would need to read them: what happened, when, and who else saw it. Not how it felt; what occurred.
  • Keep originals — messages, records, documents — and get copies somewhere no one else can reach: not a shared device, not an account someone else has the password to.
  • If you are already months or years in with nothing written down, you can still rebuild the timeline from traces you never made on purpose — bank and phone records, the dates on messages, medical and work files, other people’s memories. A gap in your notes is not the end of a record. It is the start of a reconstruction.

The full method, step by step, is in the Appendix at the back of this book. But the core of it is a single move: a record makes the pattern visible to someone who was not there — on your terms, and in advance, before the story gets decided for you. That is the one thing the machine is built to prevent, which is exactly why it is the thing to do.

How big is it — and the one honest question

It tends to come in layers. The groundwork is restriction — of your money, your movement, your contacts — because a restricted life is easier to watch. What can be watched can be arranged: situations set up, encounters engineered. What can be arranged can be used against you. And in the most resourced cases, at the very top, it can be used to manufacture an association — to make you appear to be, or to have done, something you did not.

But here is the part that protects you, and it cuts both ways: most situations are one layer, not the whole stack. The full chain is rare and expensive, because every layer added costs someone money, coordination, and time. So the honest move is to count what is actually in front of you rather than assume the whole machine. One tapped phone is one tapped phone until there is evidence of more.

This answers the question you are probably most afraid to ask — am I just paranoid? The answer is not “yes,” and it is not “no.” The answer is: you check, and the checking is your protection, not a betrayal of your own judgment. For each thing that feels wrong, ask whether it is truly the pattern, or whether it is ordinary incompetence, coincidence, or someone simply behaving badly — and require something you could show a stranger before you count it. What is real survives that question easily. What isn’t, falls away. Either way you end up holding something you can act on, instead of a fear no one else can see. Do not inflate one bad event into a whole conspiracy; and do not dismiss a real pattern because each piece, taken alone, looks small. Both mistakes end in the same place — with no one able to help you.

If you want the proof

Everything on this page is stated plainly and asks to be taken on its usefulness. The rest of the book does the opposite: it proves each piece, across four scales, from a single kitchen table to the CIA. It draws that proof out of court records, declassified files, and institutions’ own reports on themselves, so that no one can tell you these things do not happen. It is built so you can go as deep as you want and stop wherever you need to. The book does not ask to be believed. It asks to be checked.

If this page is all you have room for today, you have enough to begin. Take the safety step. Start the record. Come back for the rest when the ground will hold it.
