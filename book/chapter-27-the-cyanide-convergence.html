Part III · Chapter 27

The Cyanide Convergence

An American mob hitman and a Soviet state laboratory, an ocean apart and with no plausible connection, independently reached for the same tool — cyanide, to make a murder read as a heart attack. It is this book’s clearest illustration that the pattern is convergence on a widely known tool, not conspiracy.

This is a resemblance that was true in the world before anyone wrote a sentence about it, rather than a pattern assembled from cases selected to fit a thesis — though, as this chapter will insist, what it demonstrates is smaller than it first appears. It is worth reading the smaller cases around it first, because they establish just how difficult an arranged accident actually is to prove — which makes what happens at the center of this chapter all the more striking.

Todd Kendhammer called emergency services in September 2016 to report that his wife had been killed when “a pipe or something” came through their windshield. Forensic examiners found his own DNA under her fingernails, and the injury pattern didn’t match the crash he described; he was convicted of first-degree intentional homicide, an automatic life sentence in Wisconsin.1 Peter Wlasiuk reported that his wife’s pickup truck had veered off the road into a freezing lake; investigators found he had inquired about her insurance payout only nine hours after her death, and eyewitnesses placed him, not her, behind the wheel that evening. He was convicted of second-degree murder and sentenced to twenty-five years to life.2 Both convictions rest on the same evidentiary pattern: not disbelief of the story on its face, but one specific, concrete fact the staged scenario couldn’t account for.

A third case belongs here specifically because it complicates rather than confirms. Michael Peterson called 911 in December 2001 to report finding his wife Kathleen dead at the bottom of their staircase. Prosecutors argued her multiple head injuries were inconsistent with a simple fall and consistent with severe blunt trauma; he was convicted of first-degree murder in 2003.3 In 2011, a judge vacated that verdict and granted a new trial after finding that tainted blood-spatter evidence had been presented and that a key prosecution witness had committed perjury. Rather than face a second trial, Peterson entered an Alford plea in 2017 to a reduced charge, allowing him to maintain his innocence while acknowledging prosecutors likely had enough evidence to convict again, and was sentenced to time already served.4 This case cannot be cited as proof the staged-accident mechanism was used. What it proves instead is how genuinely difficult it is to distinguish an actual accident from a staged one, even under intense forensic and legal scrutiny, and how easily that same process can be corrupted in either direction.

At the scale of a state, the same mechanism traced through three American households becomes something a government laboratory engineers on purpose.

In 1957, the KGB trained a twenty-five-year-old agent named Bohdan Stashynsky to use a purpose-built spray gun that fired a jet of gas from a crushed cyanide capsule — engineered specifically so that the victim’s death would present as cardiac arrest, an ordinary heart attack, rather than poisoning. Stashynsky used it to kill the Ukrainian nationalist leader Lev Rebet in Munich in 1957, and an improved version of the same weapon to kill Stepan Bandera, also in Munich, in October 1959.5 In August 1961, persuaded by his wife, he walked into a West Berlin police station and confessed everything — that he was fleeing the KGB, and that he had murdered both men on its orders. He stood trial before West Germany’s Federal Court in Karlsruhe, was convicted, and served four years of an eight-year sentence.6 This is where the chapter’s real weight sits — not in the later convergence, but here. A state’s own chemists built a weapon for exactly this deception, the man who fired it confessed to a Western court in detail, and that court convicted him on his own testimony.

A related American case shows the same intelligence apparatus producing an official verdict that independent forensic evidence would later directly contradict, though without the clean resolution Stashynsky’s case provides. Frank Olson, an Army biological-warfare scientist working closely with the CIA, was covertly dosed with LSD without his knowledge or consent by CIA chemist Sidney Gottlieb at a meeting in rural Maryland in November 1953. Nine days later, he fell to his death from a window of New York’s Hotel Statler.7 The government’s official position shifted over the following decades — first suicide, later softened to “misadventure” — with no public acknowledgment of the LSD dosing until the Rockefeller Commission disclosed it in 1975, later folded into the Church Committee’s broader findings on the CIA’s non-consensual human-experimentation program, MKUltra.8 In 1994, Olson’s son had his father’s body exhumed; forensic re-examination found a previously undisclosed hematoma and evidence of blunt-force trauma to his head, described as more consistent with a blow before the fall than with a jump, and the New York District Attorney’s office reopened the case.9 No one has confessed to killing Olson, and the case remains officially unresolved even after reopening — this book cites it not as a solved murder but as a documented instance in which an official cause-of-death finding was directly contradicted by independent forensic evidence, decades after the fact, with the question never fully closed. The Church Committee’s own 1975 findings supply the institutional backdrop for both cases, and are worth citing in the Committee’s own careful language rather than flattened into a single claim: the CIA had “directly plotted” against Fidel Castro and Patrice Lumumba, had engaged in “covert activity” against Rafael Trujillo and Ngo Dinh Diem without any established direct link to their actual deaths, and had documented links to the Chilean group responsible for killing General René Schneider.10 Three different findings, three different evidentiary weights, drawn by the Committee itself.

And here is the demonstration this chapter exists to deliver. Richard Kuklinski, a New Jersey criminal associated with Gambino crime family capo Roy DeMeo, used liquid cyanide, lacing a drink or throwing it directly onto skin, and, by his own account in later interviews, a modified spray device — specifically to induce cardiac arrest, so that a killing would present as a natural heart attack.11 Kuklinski was tried and convicted for these killings, but cyanide as the method was the prosecution’s case — resting on accomplice testimony and his own recorded statements, not on toxicology, since the medical examiners never tested either body for cyanide in time. The spray-device detail comes only from his own uncorroborated later telling, the same source this book elsewhere declines to trust on his inflated victim count. The convergence does not need it. What a Soviet state chemistry lab engineered for Stashynsky’s spray gun was the same essential method an ocean away: cyanide, to make a murder read as a natural death.

The convergence is real, but why it is striking is less than “two independent inventions.” That cyanide produces rapid cardiac arrest, and can read as a heart attack, is not secret knowledge; it is standard toxicology, familiar enough to appear in suicide capsules and detective fiction. So an enforcer and a state laboratory did not each invent the idea from nothing. They converged, with no contact and no plausible common source, on the correct tool for an identical problem — how do you make a killing indistinguishable from a natural death? — because a problem with a small number of real solutions pushes unrelated actors toward the same one. That is exactly the distinction this book has insisted on from its opening pages: analogy, not connection — and here, analogy that can be shown rather than merely asserted.

Kuklinski separately experimented with a second concealment method, freezing victims’ bodies in an industrial freezer specifically to disguise time of death — the origin of his nickname, “the Iceman.” The method was discovered only when he disposed of one body before it had fully thawed on a warm night, and the coroner found chunks of ice still inside the corpse’s heart, a concrete forensic detail that shows exactly how the deception failed rather than merely asserting that it existed.12 He was arrested in December 1986 after an eighteen-month undercover operation, convicted of four murders in 1988, and sentenced to two consecutive life terms; his later claims of a hundred to two hundred total victims remain uncorroborated and are stated here as claims, not established fact.13 The murders he was convicted of, however, are a matter of court record, and it is those, not the inflated later tally, that this chapter’s argument rests on. In the killing of Gary Smith, the cyanide Kuklinski administered failed to work and the victim was strangled instead — a documented reminder that the method’s reliability was always more claimed than proven.

A structurally different mechanism operated in Sicily, answering a related but distinct question. Where Kuklinski’s method disguises the cause of death, the practice Sicilians call lupara bianca, “white shotgun,” eliminates any confirmable death at all, ensuring the victim’s body is simply never found. The investigative journalist Mauro De Mauro was kidnapped returning home from work in Palermo in September 1970; his body has never been recovered, more than five decades later.14 A more recent case, the 2022 disappearance of Carmine Zurlo, was explicitly classified as lupara bianca once investigators confirmed his disposal, though his body was likewise never found.15 Forensic-anthropology research on remains eventually recovered from a separate group, the so-called Mafia of Gargano, has confirmed murder as the cause of death for victims previously listed simply as “vanished” for decades — institutional confirmation, arriving only after the fact, of a mechanism built specifically to prevent any confirmation at all.16

A third organized-crime mechanism shows the same staging infrastructure repurposed for profit rather than personal motive, and escalating to murder only once its own survival was threatened. FBI investigation broke a staged-accident fraud ring in New Orleans that used “slammers,” drivers who deliberately caused collisions with eighteen-wheelers, and “spotters” who removed the slammer from the scene before police arrived, feeding injured victims to specific affiliated attorneys; a separate Connecticut ring staged more than fifty crashes, collecting ten to thirty thousand dollars in insurance payouts per incident.17 These rings are frequently linked to established organized-crime networks, and in one documented case, a ring murdered a member who had turned FBI witness — the accident-staging infrastructure itself producing an actual killing once informal fraud crossed into active law-enforcement cooperation risk.18

This chapter closes on a different category of evidence entirely — not a conviction, but a documented pattern of a government declining to look closely at what its own allies had already told it. BuzzFeed News’s 2017 investigation “From Russia With Blood” identified fourteen deaths on British soil — by apparent falls, hangings, and other means officially ruled accident, suicide, or natural causes — that American intelligence agencies had flagged to their British counterparts as suspected Russian state assassinations. In every one of the fourteen cases, British inquests concluded non-suspicious causes of death, and police confirmed there was no basis to reopen any of the investigations even after the intelligence linkage became public.19 One specific cluster within the pattern is worth naming directly: the businessman Scot Young died after falling from a fourth-floor window; three of his close friends and business associates had already died in apparent suicides in the four years before his own death, and all three were separately added to American intelligence files tracking suspected Russian-linked deaths.20 What this actually establishes is limited: no court has found any of these fourteen deaths to be homicide, and this is not cited as fourteen solved murders. What is documented, and in its own way more unsettling than a conviction would be, is narrower and precise — that high-grade intelligence directly contradicting an official accident or suicide ruling existed, was shared between allied governments, and was never acted upon. That is a real, reported governmental choice.

One caution belongs here more than anywhere, because this is the convergence chapter, and convergence is the rarest thing in the book. That a mob killer and a state laboratory reached for the same poison shows the mechanism is available at every scale; it does not show that the scales gather in any one life. They almost never do. This book has laid out many layers — the surveillance, the reframe, the poison, the arranged accident, the bought silence — so that each can be recognized on its own, but the catalogue is not a forecast that they arrive together. The ordinary case is one layer, sometimes two; the whole machine turned on a single person is vanishingly rare, and where it is real it leaves the most evidence of all, because every added layer charges someone money, coordination, and risk. To read the catalogue as the description of one life is to commit the very error Chapter Eleven exists to prevent. The book shows you the whole toolbox so that you can name a tool when you meet one — not so that you will see the whole toolbox in your own hands.
