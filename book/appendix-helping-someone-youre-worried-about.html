Appendix: Helping Someone You're Worried About

This appendix is not new argument. Most of this book is written for the person inside the machine. This page is for the person beside them — the friend, parent, sibling, or colleague who suspects that someone they care about is being controlled. The book has a hard warning for you in particular (Chapter 32): the same operation that isolates a victim also recruits the people around them, sincerely and in good faith, to help do it. So the first person to check is yourself. Take only what you can carry today.

First, make sure you are not a recruit

Chapter 32 documents the most reliable move against a target: not lying to the people around her, but arranging a real scene for them to witness, or reaching them with an account before she can give her own. If you already “know the story” about someone before you have heard it from them — if a partner, a parent, or a colleague supplied your view of them first — you may be testifying, in complete good faith, to something that was arranged for you to see. The test is the sequence: who reached you first, what were you shown, and were you ever given the other side? Hold your judgment until you have heard it directly from the person themselves.

Do not say “just leave”

The instinct is to push for the exit. It backfires, for reasons the book documents. Leaving is the most dangerous moment, not the safe one (Chapter 44 and the “Building a Record” appendix), so pushing someone toward it without a plan can raise the very risk you are trying to lower. And ultimatums — it’s them or me — do the isolator’s work for them: they make you one more relationship the target has to lose, which is precisely the outcome the mechanism is engineering (Chapters 9, 27). Every exit you force closes a door you could have kept open.

Be the door that stays open

The single most valuable thing an outsider can be is the one connection the machine could not take — a relationship that is not mediated, not conditional, and not withdrawn when they fail to act on your advice. Chapter 4 names exactly this: offline, unmediated relationships are the most reliable counterweight there is to a captured reality. So stay reachable. Keep contact that has no price attached to it. Say, and keep saying, some version of: I am here whether or not you do anything about this, and nothing you tell me changes that. An open door outlasts the best argument.

One caution governs all of that, and it is the mirror of the warning at the top of “Resources and Help.” The channel you reach them on may be read. A message saying I am here, whatever you decide is, on a monitored phone, a disclosure that they have someone — which is the exact fact the isolation was built to prevent, and the exact fact that gets acted on. That is not a reason to go quiet; going quiet is the outcome the machine is engineering, and the point of this page is to refuse it. It is a reason to let them set the terms. Say the open-door thing once, in person if you can, and then follow their lead on where, when, and how you talk. If they only ever reply at certain hours, or in a certain register, or never about anything that matters, treat that as information rather than as indifference — and do not make them explain it on a channel someone else may be holding.

Believe them, and help hold the record

Because the target’s own memory is under attack (Chapter 5), an outside witness who simply remembers accurately is a countermeasure in itself. Believe them; do not audit their account for consistency the way the abuser does — that reflex, from someone they trust, can feel like the same trap. Where they want it, you can help in the concrete way the book describes: be a person they can send a dated message to in real time; keep copies of things somewhere safe, outside their household; remember what you actually saw (see the “Building a Record” appendix). You are corroboration — the one thing a two-person, he-said-she-said frame is built to deny them.

Understand the freeze, and drop “why don’t they…”

The book is precise about this (Chapter 5): compliance, appeasement, and not-leaving are frequently conditioned responses, not decisions — the freeze is very often the resistance, not consent to the harm. Why don’t they just leave / say something / fight back misreads a trained nervous system as a failure of will, and the person can hear that judgment coming before you finish it. Replace the question with presence. You are not there to grade their choices; you are there to remain.

What not to do

  • Don’t confront the abuser. It tips off the operation, can escalate the danger to the person you’re trying to help, and can destroy the record before it exists (Chapter 49).
  • Don’t demand proof, or a decision, on your timeline. Recognition is slow from the inside (Chapters 4, 11). Pressing for certainty pushes them back toward the person who offers it.
  • Don’t go quiet because they didn’t listen. Withdrawal is the one outcome the isolation is built to produce. Outlast it.
  • Don’t turn detective on the abuser. Corroborate what you yourself witness; route genuine danger to professionals (see “Resources and Help”).

When it is urgent

If you believe there is immediate danger, that overrides all of the patience above. Contact local emergency services first, and know that the domestic-violence lines in “Resources and Help” will advise you, the outsider, on how to help safely — including how to act without tipping off someone who may be monitoring the person you are worried about.

You may not be able to get them out. You can refuse to be one more exit that closes — and that, far more than any argument, is what an outsider is for.
