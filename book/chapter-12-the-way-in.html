The Way In

Every operation in this book has to get inside a target’s defenses before it can do anything else, and the most reliable way in is not force but its opposite: manufactured intimacy, delivered so fast and so completely that the target opens the door themselves — and afterward cannot tell being chosen from being selected.

The name for this comes, precisely, from the people who perfected it. In the 1970s the American followers of Sun Myung Moon’s Unification Church had a term for what they did to a promising recruit, used without embarrassment: love bombing. A student approached on a campus, or a lonely young person who wandered into a workshop, would be surrounded at once by members who flattered, touched, praised, and attended to them with an intensity no ordinary friendship offers — a manufactured flood of belonging, aimed at whoever seemed most in need of it. It was not a warm-up to recruitment. It was recruitment. Moon used the phrase “love bomb” in a 1978 speech; his members had coined it to describe, approvingly, what they were already doing.1

Everything this Part has examined so far operates on someone already within reach. Isolation severs a person from anyone who might confirm reality; surveillance presumes access to the life being watched; the reframe rewrites a story already underway; the financial cage closes around shared accounts. Each is a mechanism of control, not of entry — the move that gets the operator inside the perimeter, close enough to do the rest. That move is the most disarming instrument in the book, precisely because it does not feel like one. It arrives as its own opposite: not as a threat but as being seen, not as capture but as being chosen.

At the scale of a single relationship the pattern has been documented for decades, usually under the clinical names idealization and love bombing: an opening phase of overwhelming attention — constant contact, rapid declarations, a mirroring so precise the target feels finally, uniquely understood. What separates it from ordinary early romance is not its warmth but its velocity and totality. Real intimacy accrues; manufactured intimacy is deployed, because its purpose is not to build a bond but to manufacture one fast enough to lower a person’s defenses before their judgment can catch up. This is the same intermittent-reward schedule examined earlier, run in reverse and up front: the flood of reward first, creating the attachment that its later, calculated withdrawal will exploit. Idealization and devaluation are not two different relationships. They are the front and back of one operation, and the first is what makes the second survivable.2

Where the target is a child, or a person being drawn into exploitation, the same entry move is called grooming, and researchers have mapped it as a consistent sequence: selecting a target, gaining access, building trust, filling an unmet need, isolating, and only then escalating — each stage, from the outside, indistinguishable from ordinary care.3 An adult who makes a neglected child feel singularly valued has disabled the child’s alarm before any harm is attempted. The commercial version is industrialized. In the trafficking pattern European investigators call the “loverboy” method, a recruiter manufactures a romance — devotion, gifts, a shared future — expressly to convert love into leverage, until the victim is delivered into exploitation by the very person she believes loves her.4 In each case the affection is real in its delivery and false in its purpose, and that gap is the entire design.

Run on a group rather than a person, the same move builds a cult. The Unification Church’s love bombing was a production line for exactly that individual idealization — administered by a whole community at once, far harder to disbelieve than a single ardent suitor, because the target is not courted by one person but embraced by a world.5 The most fully documented recent case carried the pattern all the way to a federal conviction. Keith Raniere’s NXIVM recruited through Executive Success Programs — self-improvement workshops promising growth and belonging — and escalated its inner circle into a secret “women-only mentorship” called DOS, which members joined believing it was a sisterhood before discovering they had sworn lifelong vows of obedience and were, in the group’s own structure, “slaves.” In 2019 a federal jury convicted Raniere of racketeering, sex trafficking, and forced-labor conspiracy, and in 2020 he was sentenced to a hundred and twenty years.6 The entry was self-help and sisterhood. The destination was coercion. The distance between them was covered one manufactured intimacy at a time.

At the top of the scale the same grammar becomes a line item in an intelligence budget, and there it even keeps the romantic vocabulary. The tradecraft term for turning a person into an asset is cultivation — the patient manufacture of a relationship, backed by a state’s research into the target’s tastes, needs, and loneliness, for the purpose of extraction. Its most literal form was the East German Stasi’s “Romeo” program: under the foreign-intelligence chief Markus Wolf, the service deployed men to court lonely secretaries inside West German ministries and turn them, over months of manufactured devotion, into sources. (The service’s most consequential penetration of the Brandt government, Günter Guillaume’s, was not a Romeo case at all but a deep-cover sleeper’s, and is routinely miscounted as one.) Wolf wrote, without shame, of having “perfected the use of sex in spying.”7 The KGB ran the same craft through operatives its officers called “swallows” and “ravens,” and the sexless version — the case officer who becomes, over years, a target’s most trusted confidant — is simply cultivation with the romance removed.8 This is the state-scale edition of the abuser’s opening month and the cult’s welcome workshop: the identical move, engineer intimacy to get inside a defended life, carried out with a government’s resources behind it.

What makes the way in the most deniable instrument in this book is that the target participates. She opens the door; she says yes; she feels, at the time, not captured but lucky. Afterward the record reads as consensual and even eager — which is exactly the cover: manufactured intimacy and the real thing are, act for act, identical on the surface. The first chapter put that problem as a rule about single acts; here it arrives as a question a person has to ask about her own life. This is the epistemic trap at its most intimate: the person it happened to cannot cleanly separate I was loved from I was cultivated, and neither can an observer reading only the surface. The answer is the same as everywhere else — reconstruct rather than believe. Cultivation leaves traces organic intimacy does not: the anomalous speed; the too-perfect mirroring of a stranger’s tastes; the attentiveness that functions, on inspection, as intelligence-gathering; the isolation that arrives dressed as devotion — I don’t want to share you; the research that precedes the “chance” encounter. What reconstruction recovers is not the warmth, which was real enough to feel. It is the tempo and the asymmetry — the evidence that one party was building a bond while the other was operating one.

This is why the way in belongs at the close of this Part rather than its opening. Only after the full apparatus is visible does its most unsettling feature come into focus: that it routinely enters a life disguised as the opposite of all of it. As being seen. As being chosen. As the luckiest thing that ever happened to a person. The door to every mechanism here is seldom forced; far more often it is opened from the inside, by someone made to feel they were opening it to love.

  2. Idealization (“love bombing”) as the opening phase of a coercive relationship, structurally paired with a later devaluation; the accelerated, high-intensity attachment it induces is the same intermittent-reinforcement and traumatic-bonding dynamic examined in this book’s opening chapters.   3. Sexual grooming as a documented, staged process (target selection, gaining access, trust development, filling a need, isolation, and escalation), each stage superficially indistinguishable from ordinary care. See the sexual-grooming research literature, e.g., Elizabeth L. Jeglic and Georgia M. Winters, Sexual Grooming: Integrating Research, Practice, Prevention, and Policy (2022).↑

  4. The “loverboy” (or “Romeo pimp”) trafficking-recruitment method, documented particularly in the Netherlands and across Europe: a manufactured romantic relationship used deliberately to recruit and then control a victim for sexual exploitation.↑

  5. Unification Church love bombing as group-administered idealization, harder to resist than a single suitor because the target is embraced by an entire community at once; see note 1 (Singer; Lifton).↑

Keith Raniere, founder of NXIVM and its Executive Success Programs, and the secret DOS sub-group whose members were recruited under the guise of a women-only mentorship before learning they had taken vows of obedience; convicted by a federal jury on June 19, 2019 of racketeering, racketeering conspiracy, sex trafficking, and forced-labor conspiracy, and sentenced in 2020 to 120 years. U.S. Department of Justice, Eastern District of New York.↑

  7.   8. KGB “swallows” (female) and “ravens” (male) trained for seduction-based operations; the non-sexual equivalent is ordinary agent cultivation — the slow manufacture of trust by a case officer. Standard in the intelligence-history literature.↑

The terms this chapter uses

What does “Reconstruction” mean?

The book’s answer to both believing invisible harm because it would explain everything and disbelieving it because no one can see it: instead, building the invisible back into visibility through whatever trace it could not help but leave, because a well-run operation can hide its cause but not fully hide its effects. Show the mechanism; show what it left behind.

What does “Traumatic bonding” mean?

Dutton and Painter’s account of what happens when a severe power imbalance is combined with intermittent reinforcement — cruelty interrupted, at unpredictable intervals, by real kindness. The not-knowing-which-is-coming wires attachment to the source of harm, so the same person is registered as both danger and comfort; the bond can outlast the relationship.
