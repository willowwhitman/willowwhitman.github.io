Part
Back matter: The practical apparatus
Length
1,240 words, about 5 minutes

The text

These terms are defined as this book uses them, which is not always how a dictionary or another field would.

Agentic state — Milgram’s term for the felt shift in which a person stops experiencing themselves as the author of an act and begins experiencing themselves as the instrument of someone else’s decision. Guilt has nowhere to attach because the self that could refuse has, for the duration, been handed over.

Analogy — Two instances of the same mechanism that resemble each other because unrelated actors converged on the same small set of methods that actually work, with no contact and no coordination between them. This is the book’s default assumption for every resemblance it documents: same method, independent actors, no link assumed. Most of what the book describes is analogy.

Anchoring and framing — Tversky and Kahneman’s finding that the first figure or frame a person is handed silently sets the reference point everything after is judged from, and that identical facts cast as a gain or a loss produce opposite choices. It is the cognitive basis for the book’s keystone move: deciding the story in advance, then fitting the facts to it.

Claim tiers (documented, synthesis, contested) — The three levels at which the book holds every major claim, never blurred together. Documented: settled by a record — a court file, a declassified document, a peer-reviewed study, a government’s report on itself. Synthesis: a pattern honestly assembled from individually documented components and argued as a case, not asserted as proven, because no single instance proves the whole chain end to end. Contested: genuinely unresolved, often because the mechanism was built to leave no trace, so the absence of proof is equally consistent with nothing having happened and with something having been done well.

Coercive control — The book’s central subject: not a personality flaw or a private tragedy, but a mechanism — isolate a target from anyone who could independently confirm reality, monopolize what the target is allowed to believe, induce helplessness, punish deviation, escalate when threatened, and deny it all in a form built to be unprovable or reclassifiable.

Coercive control as a legal offence — The recognition, in law, of the pattern itself as a crime rather than its separate acts. The UK’s Serious Crime Act 2015, s.76, was the first major common-law statute to criminalize controlling or coercive behaviour as a course of conduct — the point where the law began to catch up to the mechanism.

DARVO — Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender: Jennifer Freyd’s name for the move in which the person who caused the harm takes the victim’s chair, denies the act, attacks the accuser, and recasts the accusation itself as the injury done to him. It works best where there is no fixed record to anchor against.

Deniability engine (engineered deniability) — Deniability built directly into the design of an operation, so that whatever is done can be dismissed as an accident, an illness, a delusion, or a conspiracy theory too far-fetched to entertain. In the book’s account this is not damage control after the fact but a structural feature present from the start.

Gaslighting — The disputing of a person’s memory of events, incident by small incident, until they stop trusting their own account. The book treats it as effective not merely because someone is willing to lie but because it exploits a real property of memory — that memory is reconstructive and suggestible — which keeps the definition narrow rather than mystical.

Grammar and vocabulary — The book’s distinction between what changes between cases and what does not. Vocabulary is the particular instrument an operator happens to use — poison, surveillance, a psychiatric label, a disinformation campaign. Grammar is the constant structure underneath: intent to control; isolation, monopolized reality, induced helplessness, and escalation when threatened; and deniability engineered into the design. Different actors speak different vocabularies while running the same grammar.

Herman’s three stages — Judith Herman’s recovery framework, which the book holds cannot be skipped or reordered: first safety, because a nervous system still detecting threat cannot do any other work; then remembrance and mourning, building an honest narrative of what happened; then reconnection, rebuilding relationships, meaning, and the restored capacity to make choices that change outcomes.

Homology — A documented, provable connection between two instances of the same mechanism — not merely the same method appearing independently, but an actual link established by evidence. The book holds this to a far higher bar than analogy and asserts it only where the evidence forces the conclusion, naming such cases as the exceptions they are rather than folding them into the general pattern.

Illusory-truth effect — The finding that a statement encountered more than once is judged more likely to be true on the strength of the repetition alone, and that this operates even against knowledge the person could use to flag the claim as false. It is the engine under a repeated version of events, a chanted slogan, and saturation propaganda alike.

Learned helplessness — Seligman and Maier’s finding, as reframed by later neuroscience: prolonged, genuinely uncontrollable stress wears down the brain circuit that detects whether a situation is escapable, so that a real exit can go unregistered. The book uses it to turn why didn’t she just leave from a character judgment into a neurological one.

Mere-exposure effect — Zajonc’s finding that familiarity alone produces a drift toward trusting whatever has become familiar, with no new reason supplied. The book’s point is that repeated, unpressured contact is not a neutral prelude to influence but is already influence — which is why grooming so often begins with nothing more than presence.

Misinformation effect — Loftus’s documentation that a misleading detail introduced after an event can be folded into memory as though witnessed, and that whole false episodes can be implanted through confident, repeated suggestion from a trusted source. The book is careful that this is not the claim that memory is worthless, only that it is reconstructive and suggestible, most of all under stress and dependency.

Reconstruction — The book’s answer to both believing invisible harm because it would explain everything and disbelieving it because no one can see it: instead, building the invisible back into visibility through whatever trace it could not help but leave, because a well-run operation can hide its cause but not fully hide its effects. Show the mechanism; show what it left behind.

Scale-invariance — The property that the mechanism of coercive control runs the same way at every scale — one relationship, a closed organization, an institution that looks away, a state with intelligence tradecraft behind it. What changes climbing between scales is never the method, only how much money, manpower, and legal cover the operator has to make the operation deniable.

Traumatic bonding — Dutton and Painter’s account of what happens when a severe power imbalance is combined with intermittent reinforcement — cruelty interrupted, at unpredictable intervals, by real kindness. The not-knowing-which-is-coming wires attachment to the source of harm, so the same person is registered as both danger and comfort; the bond can outlast the relationship.

Zersetzung — The Stasi’s “decomposition,” codified in MfS Directive 1/76: covert operations aimed at secretly destroying a target’s self-confidence, reputation, employment, and relationships until the will to resist collapsed, all without a visible state hand. The book reads it as an institutional, months- or years-long version of the inescapable-stress condition that produces learned helplessness.
