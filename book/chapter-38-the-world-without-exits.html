The World Without Exits

A closed group manufactures a member’s entire world, runs the breaking-engine from inside it, and installs a lock — collateral, shunning, an oath, a blood oath — that makes leaving cost more than staying. Cult, church, fraternity, crime family: one machine, five names.

The most complete version of coercive control is a group that manufactures a member’s entire world from scratch and runs the engine of Chapter Two on them from inside it. A few kinds of group do this, usually treated as distinct — the cult, lurid and disreputable; the oath-bound society, respectable and often admired; and, between them, the high-control church, borrowing the respectability of the one and the totality of the other. They are the same machine. Only the vocabulary differs.

Start with the cult, and with the case that carries a federal conviction. NXIVM was marketed for two decades as an executive self-improvement company. Inside it, founder Keith Raniere ran a secret society called DOS: women recruited under the pretense of a female-empowerment mentorship learned only afterward that they had sworn lifelong “vows of obedience” as “slaves” to “masters,” Raniere atop every chain. Membership required handing over “collateral”: sexually explicit photographs, damaging confessions, deeds to property, rights over one’s own children — material whose only function was to make leaving, or talking, unsurvivable. Members were kept on near-starvation diets and chronic sleep deprivation, and the innermost were branded near the pelvis with a cauterizing pen, in a design incorporating Raniere’s initials.1 Read that inventory against Chapter Two and nothing is left over: isolation, reality monopolized by one unquestionable authority, debility through hunger and exhaustion, degradation, and, in the collateral, a purpose-built lock turning Biderman’s ordinary threat into a permanent, self-tightening one. In 2019 a federal jury convicted Raniere of racketeering, sex trafficking, forced labor conspiracy, and related crimes; in 2020 he was sentenced to a hundred and twenty years.2 The cult is not a metaphor for coercive control. It is coercive control incorporated, with bylaws.

The end state, when nothing checks the mechanism, is a group whose only permitted exit is death — and this too is documented rather than theorized. Jim Jones moved his Peoples Temple to an isolated Guyanese jungle compound specifically to sever his followers from every competing source of reality, and rehearsed mass suicide in drills he called “White Nights.” On November 18, 1978, after his people murdered a visiting U.S. congressman who had come to help members leave, he oversaw the deaths of more than nine hundred followers, a third of them children.3 The pattern recurs with no contact between its instances: Heaven’s Gate’s thirty-nine members died together in 1997 believing they were shedding their bodies to board a spacecraft, and Aum Shinrikyo, having sealed its members inside a total alternative reality, manufactured and released nerve gas on the Tokyo subway in 1995 — a cult wielding a capability otherwise documented at the scale of states.4 The sociologist Janja Lalich named what makes such endings possible from the inside: not brainwashing in the comic-book sense, but “bounded choice,” a condition in which a person keeps the feeling of choosing freely while the boundaries of the thinkable have been drawn so tightly that every available choice serves the group.5 The member is not a puppet. They are a person choosing, sincerely, from a menu written entirely by someone else.

You need no jungle compound or branding iron to run this. The same engine operates, at vastly larger scale, inside organizations the law recognizes as ordinary churches — and its exit-lock has a name most members know: being cut off. The Jehovah’s Witnesses practice disfellowshipping, under which a member who is expelled or who formally leaves is shunned by every Witness they know, immediate family included, so that departure costs not a friendship or two but an entire social world at once. When Norway moved to withhold state funding over the practice, its own district court found in 2024 that the exclusion arrangement drove departing members into documented social isolation — a government naming, in flat administrative prose, the first technique on Biderman’s chart, though an appeal court reversed that ruling in 2025 and the litigation may yet continue.6 The Church of Scientology runs the identical mechanism as disconnection: a member must sever all contact with anyone the organization labels a “suppressive person” — a designation documented to reach members’ own parents, spouses, and children the moment those relatives criticize the church.7 The claim here is deliberately narrow, because the opposite would be false: religion is not coercion, and the overwhelming majority of it is nothing of the kind. But where this specific engine is present, a tax-exempt, legally protected institution can run it on millions with a deniability no cult or crime family can approach, the whole apparatus shielded as sincere faith and administered as love. Shunning is NXIVM’s collateral in a lower key — a lock that makes leaving cost everyone the member has.

Now change the vocabulary and keep the grammar, and the same machine appears inside institutions no one calls cults. A fraternity or university marching band is an oath-bound society that recruits, isolates a pledge in an intense in-group, escalates demands from trivial to degrading, and secures lifelong silence — coercion documented in court records because it has killed. Robert Champion, a drum major in Florida A&M University’s band, was beaten to death in 2011 running a hazing gauntlet called “Crossing Bus C”; multiple band members were convicted of manslaughter and hazing.8 Timothy Piazza died at a Penn State fraternity in 2017 after a pledging ritual; its officers were prosecuted, but the most serious charges were dismissed and most defendants pleaded to hazing and alcohol counts — the mechanism plainly real, yet still slipping a legal net built for other crimes. What binds these societies is the same lock in a lower key: pledges describe “an oath of silence, a bond,” and the fear of being targeted, or of losing the belonging just bought at such cost, keeps them quiet. The escalating demand, the degradation, the enforced silence — Biderman’s chart with school colors on it.

The binding lock is the through-line, and what distinguishes a closed group from a mere community. Every group here makes leaving cost more than staying — the collateral, the shunning, the doomsday conviction that the outside is death or damnation, the oath of silence, and, at the criminal end, the mafia’s blood oath, where the induction is a killing and the only exit is another.9 It is the engine of Chapter Two converted from process into membership condition: isolation, monopolized reality, and dread stop being done to the member and become things he enforces on himself.

That lock is also where the closed group becomes legible from outside. NXIVM’s collateral became exhibits in a federal trafficking trial; the branding was photographed; the shunning and disconnection policies are written doctrines an outside authority can read back to their own authors. The member inside bounded choice cannot see the boundary — but the mechanism that draws it leaves a record, and names the coercion she could not name for herself.

Chapter Forty-Four takes up the same oath-bound structures as a recurring form — why they keep reappearing across the world, and how a few reach into real institutional power.10 That question faces outward; this one has faced inward, at the lock itself: the collateral sealed against the day a member might talk, the initials burned in near the pelvis, the departing member shunned by everyone she has.

And yet the lock is not a sentence. People do leave these groups — walk out of the marriage, the congregation, the compound — and build lives on the other side of it; former members of every kind of group named in this chapter have done exactly that, most of them with the help of people trained for this precise passage: cult-recovery specialists, domestic-violence advocates, the services gathered at the back of this book. The lock is engineered to make leaving feel impossible from the inside, which is exactly why the plain fact that others have opened it, from that same inside, belongs here beside the machinery. What the lock cannot survive is being seen for what it is — and being seen is where an exit begins.

The terms this chapter uses

What does “Coercive control” mean?

The book’s central subject: not a personality flaw or a private tragedy, but a mechanism — isolate a target from anyone who could independently confirm reality, monopolize what the target is allowed to believe, induce helplessness, punish deviation, escalate when threatened, and deny it all in a form built to be unprovable or reclassifiable.
